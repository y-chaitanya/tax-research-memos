# Memorandum 2 — §179 Expensing vs. Bonus Depreciation: Equipment Placed in Service

**Prepared by:** Chaitanya Yarlagadda, EA
**Date:** September 2026
**Tax year:** 2026

---

## Facts

Client operates a machine shop as a single-member LLC taxed as a sole
proprietorship, filing Schedule C, and is a California resident filing Form 540.

During 2026 the client placed in service:

| Asset | Cost | Recovery period |
|---|---|---|
| CNC milling machine (new) | $185,000 | 7-year |
| Delivery van, over 6,000 lbs GVWR, 100% business use | $62,000 | 5-year |
| Office furniture (used, acquired from unrelated party) | $9,000 | 7-year |

Total placed in service: **$256,000**.

All three assets were acquired from unrelated parties. Placed-in-service dates
are to be confirmed; this memorandum assumes each was acquired and placed in
service after January 19, 2025, and that no more than 40% of the aggregate
basis was placed in service in the fourth quarter, so that the half-year
convention applies to any asset remaining on MACRS.

Net business income before any depreciation deduction is $140,000. The client
has no other trade or business income. Spouse has W-2 wages of $95,000; they
file jointly.

The client expects business income to grow substantially over the next three
years and is in a lower marginal bracket this year than they anticipate being
in later.

## Issue

Should the client elect §179 expensing, claim bonus depreciation under §168(k),
or use some combination? What order applies, what limits apply to the van, and
how does California treat each?

## Rule

### §179 expensing

§179 permits a taxpayer to elect to treat the cost of qualifying property as an
expense rather than a capital expenditure.

For tax years beginning in 2026, the maximum §179 deduction is $2,560,000,
reduced dollar for dollar once qualifying property placed in service during the
year exceeds $4,090,000, phasing out completely at $6,650,000. These are the
annually indexed amounts; the governing revenue procedure should be cited in
the workpapers.

Four features matter here:

1. **Elective and asset-specific.** The taxpayer chooses which assets to expense
   and how much of each.
2. **Taxable income limitation, §179(b)(3).** The deduction cannot exceed
   aggregate taxable income derived from the active conduct of any trade or
   business. It cannot create or increase a loss. Any disallowed amount carries
   forward.
3. **Wages are included in that limitation.** Reg. §1.179-2(c)(6)(iv) provides
   that taxable income derived from the active conduct of a trade or business
   includes wages, salaries, tips, and other compensation earned as an employee.
   On a joint return, the limitation is applied to the spouses' aggregate amount.
4. **Vehicle cap, §179(b)(5).** The §179 deduction on a sport utility vehicle
   rated between 6,000 and 14,000 lbs gross vehicle weight is capped at a fixed,
   annually indexed dollar amount. §179(b)(5)(B) defines the term broadly — any
   four-wheeled vehicle primarily designed to carry passengers — and then
   excepts, among others, a vehicle with a cargo area of at least six feet of
   interior length not readily accessible from the passenger compartment, and a
   vehicle with an integral enclosure fully enclosing the driver compartment and
   load carrying device with no seating behind the driver.

### Bonus depreciation

§168(k) allows an additional first-year depreciation deduction. For qualified
property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, the rate is 100%.

Two features matter here:

1. **Automatic unless elected out.** Bonus applies by default. An election out
   is made by class of property under §168(k)(7), not asset by asset.
2. **No taxable income limitation.** Bonus depreciation can create or increase a
   net operating loss.

Used property qualifies for bonus depreciation if it meets the acquisition
requirements of §168(k)(2)(E)(ii), which generally require that the property was
not previously used by the taxpayer and was not acquired from a related party.

§179(b)(5) is a §179 limitation only. It does not restrict bonus depreciation.

### Ordering

§179 is applied first, to the assets the taxpayer selects. Bonus depreciation
then applies to the remaining basis of eligible property. MACRS depreciation
applies to whatever basis remains after both.

### California

California does not conform to federal bonus depreciation, and its §179 limit is
$25,000 with the investment phase-out beginning at $200,000 — substantially
below the federal amounts. California depreciation must be computed separately,
which creates a federal-state difference that carries forward for the life of
each asset. Confirm both figures against current Franchise Tax Board guidance.

## Analysis

### The taxable income limitation binds — but higher than the business alone

Net business income before depreciation is $140,000. Under §179(b)(3), the
deduction is capped at aggregate taxable income derived from the active conduct
of any trade or business.

That measure is not limited to the Schedule C. Wages earned as an employee are
included under Reg. §1.179-2(c)(6)(iv), and on a joint return the spouses'
amounts aggregate. The ceiling is therefore approximately **$235,000**, not
$140,000.

One refinement matters. The limitation is computed after depreciation otherwise
allowable and before the §179 deduction itself, so bonus depreciation claimed on
assets *not* expensed under §179 reduces the same ceiling against which §179 is
measured. Electing bonus on the van and §179 on the machine is therefore not two
independent decisions — the first narrows the room available for the second.

Even at $235,000, a full §179 election of $256,000 exceeds the ceiling. The
excess is disallowed currently and carried forward under §179(b)(3)(B).

So the conclusion holds — §179 alone cannot absorb the full cost — but the
shortfall is roughly $21,000, not $116,000. That difference decides which
combinations are worth modelling at all.

### Bonus depreciation has no such ceiling

At 100%, bonus depreciation on all three assets would produce a $256,000
deduction against $140,000 of business income, creating a loss of $116,000. That
loss would offset the spouse's wages on the joint return, subject to the excess
business loss limitation of §461(l).

At $116,000, the loss is well below the threshold for married taxpayers filing
jointly, which is several times that amount. §461(l) does not bind on these
facts. It would matter only if losses from other activities were added to it.
Confirm the indexed threshold for 2026 before relying on this.

So the mechanical answer is that bonus alone gets the full deduction this year.
The planning question is whether the client wants it.

### Whether the client should take the full deduction

The client expects higher income and a higher marginal rate in future years. A
deduction is worth the marginal rate it offsets. Accelerating $256,000 into a
lower-rate year and leaving future higher-rate years with no depreciation shield
may cost more than it saves.

Three levers exist:

1. Elect out of bonus for one or more classes under §168(k)(7), spreading those
   assets over their MACRS recovery periods.
2. Elect §179 selectively on chosen assets up to the income limitation, leaving
   other assets on MACRS.
3. Take the full deduction if current cash flow matters more than rate arbitrage.

The election out of bonus is made by class, not by asset. The van is 5-year
property; the CNC machine and furniture are both 7-year. So electing out of
7-year property affects both the machine and the furniture together. The client
cannot elect out for the machine while keeping bonus on the furniture.

### A deduction is worth the rate it offsets — and it offsets QBI too

The Schedule C income is qualified business income under §199A. Depreciation
reduces QBI, so a dollar of accelerated depreciation does not save the full
marginal rate. It also removes roughly twenty cents of §199A deduction, and the
§199A benefit given up this year is not recovered in a later year.

At these numbers the effect is not marginal. Before depreciation, QBI is
$140,000 and the §199A deduction is up to $28,000, subject to the overall
taxable income limitation. Full expensing drives QBI to zero and the §199A
deduction with it. Electing out of 7-year property leaves QBI positive and
preserves part of the deduction.

A second §199A point runs the other way. The wage and capital limitation of
§199A(b)(2)(B) uses unadjusted basis immediately after acquisition, which is not
reduced by §179 or by bonus depreciation. The elections therefore change QBI but
not UBIA. That is favourable, and it matters as income grows toward the
threshold at which the limitation begins to apply. Taxable income on these facts
appears to be below that threshold, so the wage and capital limitation should
not apply for 2026; confirm the indexed threshold.

Self-employment tax points the same direction as the client's instinct. The
deduction reduces net earnings from self-employment as well as taxable income,
so a current-year deduction carries an SE tax saving that a future-year
deduction at a higher income tax rate may not fully replace. Model it; do not
assume it.

### The van: §280F is not the only cap

The van is rated over 6,000 lbs GVWR, so it falls outside the passenger
automobile limitations of §280F(d)(5). That does not end the analysis.

§179(b)(5) separately caps the §179 deduction on a vehicle within its
definition, and a delivery van is not automatically outside that definition. The
exceptions in §179(b)(5)(B) turn on cargo area length and enclosure, which are
specifications of the particular vehicle, not consequences of the word "van" in
the client's description. Obtain the manufacturer's specifications before
relying on an unlimited §179 deduction for it.

If the van is caught, the cap is a §179 cap only. Bonus depreciation under
§168(k) is not limited by §179(b)(5), so full first-year expensing would still
be available through bonus — which changes the recommended route for that asset
without changing the total available this year.

### The used furniture

Used property is eligible for bonus depreciation provided the acquisition
requirements are met. The furniture was acquired from an unrelated party and not
previously used by the client, so it appears eligible. Confirm the seller is not
a related party under §179(d)(2) and §267 before relying on this.

### California divergence

California's treatment differs from federal on both provisions. The practical
consequence is that the client will carry a different basis in each asset for
California purposes, producing a state adjustment every year until each asset is
fully depreciated or disposed of.

This matters beyond the return itself. A client who accelerates aggressively for
federal purposes takes on a multi-year reconciliation burden at the state level.
The federal saving is real, but so is the ongoing complexity, and the client
should understand that before the election is made.

## Conclusion

1. The §179 taxable income ceiling is approximately $235,000, not $140,000,
   because Reg. §1.179-2(c)(6)(iv) includes the spouse's wages. §179 alone still
   cannot absorb the full $256,000, but the shortfall is about $21,000.
2. Bonus depreciation at 100% can absorb the full amount and create a loss.
   §461(l) does not bind at a $116,000 loss.
3. Confirm whether the van meets a §179(b)(5)(B) exception before assuming an
   unlimited §179 deduction on it. If it does not, route that asset through
   bonus rather than §179.
4. Model each scenario on an after-§199A basis. A comparison of marginal rates
   alone overstates the value of accelerating, because the deduction reduces QBI
   in the same year it reduces income.
5. Model at least three scenarios: full bonus; elect out of 7-year property;
   §179 to the income limitation with MACRS on the rest.
6. Compute California separately for every asset, and explain the multi-year
   state adjustment to the client before making the federal elections.

## What would change the answer

- If the client expected lower future income, accelerating everything into 2026
  would be clearly preferable.
- If the furniture had been acquired from a related party, it would fail the
  bonus acquisition requirements and the §179 related-party rules.
- If the van were 6,000 lbs GVWR or under, the luxury auto limitations of §280F
  would cap the deduction regardless of which provision is elected.
- If the van does not meet a §179(b)(5)(B) exception, the §179 deduction on it
  is capped at the indexed amount and bonus becomes the only route to full
  first-year expensing for that asset.
- If more than 40% of the aggregate basis were placed in service in the fourth
  quarter, the mid-quarter convention would apply to any asset left on MACRS,
  changing the comparison against the elections.
- If business use of any asset fell to 50% or below in a later year, §179
  recapture under §179(d)(10) would apply.
