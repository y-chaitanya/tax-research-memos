# Memorandum 2 — §179 Expensing vs. Bonus Depreciation: Equipment Placed in Service

**Prepared by:** Chaitanya Yarlagadda, EA
**Date:** September 2026
**Tax year:** 2026

---

## Facts

Client operates a machine shop as a single-member LLC taxed as a sole proprietorship, filing Schedule C, and is a California resident filing Form 540.

During 2026 the client placed in service:

| Asset | Cost | Recovery period |
|---|---|---|
| CNC milling machine (new) | $185,000 | 7-year |
| Delivery van, over 6,000 lbs GVWR, 100% business use | $62,000 | 5-year |
| Office furniture (used, acquired from unrelated party) | $9,000 | 7-year |

Total placed in service: $256,000.

Net business income before any depreciation deduction is **$140,000**. The client has no other trade or business income. Spouse has W-2 wages of $95,000; they file jointly.

The client expects business income to grow substantially over the next three years and is in a lower marginal bracket this year than they anticipate being in later.

## Issue

Should the client elect §179 expensing, claim bonus depreciation under §168(k), or use some combination? What order applies, and how does California treat each?

## Rule

### §179 expensing

§179 permits a taxpayer to elect to treat the cost of qualifying property as an expense rather than a capital expenditure.

For tax years beginning in 2026, the maximum §179 deduction is **$2,560,000**, reduced dollar for dollar once qualifying property placed in service during the year exceeds **$4,090,000**, phasing out completely at **$6,650,000**.

Two features matter here:

- **Elective and asset-specific.** The taxpayer chooses which assets to expense and how much of each.
- **Taxable income limitation, §179(b)(3).** The deduction cannot exceed aggregate taxable income derived from the active conduct of any trade or business. It cannot create or increase a loss. Any disallowed amount carries forward.

### Bonus depreciation

§168(k) allows an additional first-year depreciation deduction. For qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, the rate is **100%**.

Two features matter here:

- **Automatic unless elected out.** Bonus applies by default. An election out is made by class of property under §168(k)(7), not asset by asset.
- **No taxable income limitation.** Bonus depreciation can create or increase a net operating loss.

Used property qualifies for bonus depreciation if it meets the acquisition requirements of §168(k)(2)(E)(ii), which generally require that the property was not previously used by the taxpayer and was not acquired from a related party.

### Ordering

§179 is applied first, to the assets the taxpayer selects. Bonus depreciation then applies to the remaining basis of eligible property. MACRS depreciation applies to whatever basis remains after both.

### California

California does not conform to federal bonus depreciation, and its §179 limits are substantially lower than the federal amounts. California depreciation must be computed separately, which creates a book-tax-state difference that carries forward for the life of each asset.

## Analysis

### The taxable income limitation is the binding constraint

Net business income before depreciation is $140,000. Under §179(b)(3), the §179 deduction is limited to taxable income from the active conduct of a trade or business.

A §179 election of the full $256,000 would exceed that limit. The excess would be disallowed currently and carried forward under §179(b)(3)(B).

Note that the limitation looks to aggregate trade or business income. Whether the spouse's W-2 wages enter that computation on a joint return depends on the treatment of wages as income derived from the active conduct of a trade or business, which should be confirmed against current guidance before the return is prepared. This is the kind of detail where the difference between a carryforward and a current deduction turns on a definition.

### Bonus depreciation has no such ceiling

At 100%, bonus depreciation on all three assets would produce a $256,000 deduction against $140,000 of business income, creating a loss of $116,000. That loss would offset the spouse's wages on the joint return, subject to the excess business loss limitation of §461(l), which should be checked against the current threshold.

So the mechanical answer is that bonus alone gets the full deduction this year. The planning question is whether the client wants it.

### Whether the client should take the full deduction

The client expects higher income and a higher marginal rate in future years. A deduction is worth the marginal rate it offsets. Accelerating $256,000 into a lower-rate year and leaving future higher-rate years with no depreciation shield may cost more than it saves.

Three levers exist:

1. **Elect out of bonus for one or more classes** under §168(k)(7), spreading those assets over their MACRS recovery periods.
2. **Elect §179 selectively** on chosen assets up to the income limitation, leaving other assets on MACRS.
3. **Take the full deduction** if current cash flow matters more than rate arbitrage.

The election out of bonus is made by class, not by asset. The van is 5-year property; the CNC machine and furniture are both 7-year. So electing out of 7-year property affects both the machine and the furniture together. The client cannot elect out for the machine while keeping bonus on the furniture.

### The used furniture

Used property is eligible for bonus depreciation provided the acquisition requirements are met. The furniture was acquired from an unrelated party and not previously used by the client, so it appears eligible. Confirm the seller is not a related party under §179(d)(2) and §267 before relying on this.

### California divergence

California's treatment differs from federal on both provisions. The practical consequence is that the client will carry a different basis in each asset for California purposes, producing a state adjustment every year until each asset is fully depreciated or disposed of.

This matters beyond the return itself. A client who accelerates aggressively for federal purposes takes on a multi-year reconciliation burden at the state level. The federal saving is real, but so is the ongoing complexity, and the client should understand that before the election is made.

## Conclusion

1. **§179 alone cannot absorb the full $256,000** because the taxable income limitation under §179(b)(3) caps it at trade or business income, which is $140,000 before depreciation.
2. **Bonus depreciation at 100% can absorb the full amount** and create a loss, subject to §461(l).
3. **The right answer is a planning decision, not a mechanical one.** Given the client's expectation of higher future rates, model at least three scenarios: full bonus, elect out of 7-year property, and §179 to the income limitation with MACRS on the rest.
4. **Compute California separately** for every asset, and explain the multi-year state adjustment to the client before making the federal elections.

## What would change the answer

- If the client expected *lower* future income, accelerating everything into 2026 would be clearly preferable.
- If the furniture had been acquired from a related party, it would fail the bonus acquisition requirements and the §179 related-party rules.
- If the van were 6,000 lbs GVWR or under, the luxury auto limitations of §280F would cap the deduction regardless of which provision is elected.
- If business use of any asset fell to 50% or below in a later year, §179 recapture under §179(d)(10) would apply.

## Open items

- Confirm treatment of spouse's W-2 wages in the §179(b)(3) taxable income computation
- Confirm current California §179 limit and bonus conformity
- Verify GVWR on the van and substantiate 100% business use
- Confirm the furniture seller is unrelated under §267
