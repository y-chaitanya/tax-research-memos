# Memorandum 3 — Home Office Deduction: Schedule C Consultant

**Prepared by:** Chaitanya Yarlagadda, EA
**Date:** September 2026
**Tax year:** 2026

---

## Facts

Client is a self-employed management consultant reporting on Schedule C. She works from a 2,000 square foot home she owns in California.

The home includes a 240 square foot converted bedroom used as her office. It contains a desk, two monitors, a printer, client files in a locked cabinet, and a bookshelf. The room has no bed and no television. Her family does not use the room, though her daughter occasionally does homework at the desk on weekends.

She meets clients at their offices, not at her home. Administrative and management work — proposals, billing, scheduling, research, and file maintenance — is done in the home office. She has no other fixed location where she conducts those activities.

2026 whole-home expenses:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Mortgage interest | $14,000 |
| Property taxes | $6,500 |
| Homeowners insurance | $2,100 |
| Utilities | $3,800 |
| Repairs (whole home) | $1,600 |
| Depreciable basis of home, excluding land | $480,000 |

Schedule C net income before the home office deduction is **$96,000**.

## Issue

Does the 240 square foot room qualify for the home office deduction under §280A, and if so, should the client use the simplified method or actual expenses?

## Rule

§280A(a) generally disallows deductions for the business use of a dwelling unit used as a residence.

§280A(c)(1) provides an exception for a portion of the dwelling used **exclusively** and on a **regular basis** as either:

- the principal place of business for any trade or business of the taxpayer, or
- a place of business used by patients, clients, or customers in meeting with the taxpayer in the normal course of business.

§280A(c)(1) further provides that a home office qualifies as the **principal place of business** if it is used for administrative or management activities of the trade or business and there is no other fixed location where the taxpayer conducts substantial administrative or management activities.

**Exclusive use** means the space is used only for business. The Tax Court has applied this strictly; incidental personal use of the space can defeat the deduction.

**Gross income limitation, §280A(c)(5).** The deduction cannot exceed gross income from the business use of the home, reduced by allocable mortgage interest, taxes, and casualty losses, and by business deductions not allocable to the home. Disallowed amounts carry forward.

**Simplified method.** Rev. Proc. 2013-13 permits a safe harbor deduction of $5 per square foot of qualified business use, capped at 300 square feet, for a maximum of $1,500. Under the simplified method, no depreciation is claimed on the home office and mortgage interest and property taxes are deducted in full on Schedule A rather than allocated.

## Analysis

### Regular use

The client works from the office daily in the conduct of her consulting practice. Regular use is satisfied without difficulty.

### Exclusive use — the issue that decides the engagement

The room is furnished as an office and contains no personal-use furnishings. That is favorable.

The problem is the daughter's weekend homework.

§280A(c)(1) requires the portion of the dwelling to be used **exclusively** for business. There is no de minimis exception in the statute for occasional personal use, and the Tax Court has denied the deduction where personal use of the claimed space was established.

Two points to raise with the client:

1. **The facts as stated put the deduction at risk.** The safest course is to stop the personal use going forward and document that the room is business-only.
2. **The exclusivity requirement applies to the space, not to the whole room as a matter of law.** A taxpayer may identify a separately identifiable portion of a room used exclusively for business, though a partition is not required. If the daughter's use is confined to a specific area, the client could claim only the exclusively used portion. That reduces the square footage but preserves the deduction.

This is a facts-and-documentation problem, not a legal one. It needs to be resolved with the client before the return is prepared, and the resolution should be recorded in the file.

### Principal place of business

Assuming exclusivity is resolved, the office qualifies as the principal place of business.

The client does not meet clients at home, so the meeting-place prong of §280A(c)(1) is unavailable. But the administrative-or-management-activities prong applies directly: she performs those activities in the home office and has no other fixed location where she performs them. That she delivers services at client sites does not defeat the deduction.

### Simplified method vs. actual expenses

Business use percentage: 240 ÷ 2,000 = **12%**.

**Simplified method.** 240 square feet × $5 = **$1,200**.

**Actual expense method**, allocating the indirect expenses at 12%:

| Item | Whole home | 12% allocable |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage interest | $14,000 | $1,680 |
| Property taxes | $6,500 | $780 |
| Insurance | $2,100 | $252 |
| Utilities | $3,800 | $456 |
| Repairs | $1,600 | $192 |
| Depreciation ($480,000 × 12% ÷ 39 years) | — | $1,477 |
| **Total** | | **$4,837** |

Actual expenses produce roughly **$3,600 more** in deduction than the simplified method.

Two qualifications on that comparison, and both matter:

**The interest and tax portion is not incremental.** The $1,680 of mortgage interest and $780 of property taxes are allocated *away* from Schedule A under the actual method. If the client itemizes, she loses those amounts there and gains them on Schedule C. The real benefit of moving them is the self-employment tax saving, not the income tax saving, and the property tax allocation interacts with the SALT limitation. If she takes the standard deduction, moving them to Schedule C is a genuine gain.

**Depreciation creates a future cost.** Depreciation claimed on the home office is subject to recapture as unrecaptured §1250 gain on sale, taxed at up to 25%, and it is not eligible for the §121 exclusion to the extent of post-May 6, 1997 depreciation. A client planning to sell in the near term should weigh a current-year deduction at her marginal rate against recapture later.

**Gross income limitation.** Schedule C net income of $96,000 is far above the $4,837 deduction, so §280A(c)(5) does not limit the deduction this year.

### Recommendation

Use the **actual expense method**, on the condition that the exclusive use issue is resolved first. The additional deduction is meaningful, and at this income level the gross income limitation is not a constraint.

If the client is likely to sell the home within a few years, or if the recordkeeping burden concerns her, the simplified method is a defensible choice that trades roughly $3,600 of current deduction for no depreciation recapture and far less substantiation.

## Conclusion

1. **Regular use is satisfied.** The office qualifies as the principal place of business under the administrative-or-management-activities prong of §280A(c)(1).
2. **Exclusive use is at risk** because of the daughter's weekend use. Resolve this with the client before claiming the deduction — either eliminate the personal use and document it, or claim only the separately identifiable portion used exclusively for business.
3. **Actual expenses produce approximately $4,837** versus $1,200 under the simplified method, based on 12% business use.
4. **Recommend actual expenses**, with the client advised of the depreciation recapture consequence on a future sale and the Schedule A reallocation of interest and taxes.

## What would change the answer

- If the client met clients at the home office, the meeting-place prong would provide an independent basis for the deduction.
- If Schedule C income were near or below the allocable expenses, §280A(c)(5) would limit the current deduction and create a carryforward.
- If the client rented rather than owned, there would be no depreciation and no recapture, and the rent would be allocated at 12%.
- If the client had another fixed location where she performed administrative work, the principal place of business prong would fail.

## Open items

- Resolve and document the exclusive use question with the client
- Confirm whether the client itemizes or takes the standard deduction, which changes the value of the interest and tax reallocation
- Determine the client's expected holding period for the home
- Obtain the allocation of purchase price between land and building to confirm depreciable basis
