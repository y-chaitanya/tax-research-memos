## Facts

The taxpayer engaged a credentialed tax preparer to prepare and electronically transmit their federal income tax return. The taxpayer timely executed Form 8879 (IRS e-file Signature Authorization), authorizing the preparer to transmit the return to the IRS. The preparer never successfully transmitted the return, and the taxpayer received no acknowledgment of acceptance. The return reached the IRS only after the statutory filing deadline had passed. The IRS assessed a failure-to-file penalty under § 6651(a)(1) of the Internal Revenue Code.

## Issue

Does a taxpayer's reliance on a professional tax preparer to electronically transmit a return constitute "reasonable cause" to escape the failure-to-file penalty under IRC § 6651(a)(1) when the preparer fails to transmit the return before the deadline?

## Rule

**1. Internal Revenue Code § 6651(a)(1).** Imposes a penalty for failure to file a tax return on or before the prescribed due date, "unless it is shown that such failure is due to reasonable cause and not due to willful neglect."

**2. Treasury Regulation § 301.6651-1(c)(1).** Defines the standard for reasonable cause. The taxpayer must demonstrate that they "exercised ordinary business care and prudence and [were] nevertheless unable to file the return within the prescribed time."

**3. *United States v. Boyle*, 469 U.S. 241 (1985).** Establishes the controlling bright-line distinction regarding reliance on an agent:

- *The bright-line rule.* The Supreme Court held that the obligation to file a tax return on time is a fixed, non-delegable, ministerial duty. Relying on an attorney or accountant to meet a filing deadline does not constitute reasonable cause if the agent misses the date, as no specialized legal or financial expertise is required to know when a deadline exists.
- *The preserved exception.* Reliance on an agent may constitute reasonable cause where the taxpayer relies on substantive professional advice regarding a question of law, such as whether a return is required to be filed at all.

**4. *Lee v. United States*, 84 F.4th 1271 (11th Cir. 2023).** Extends the *Boyle* non-delegable duty standard to the electronic filing era. The Eleventh Circuit held that executing Form 8879 merely authorizes a professional to transmit the return but does not discharge the taxpayer's personal, non-delegable obligation to confirm that transmission actually occurred and was accepted by the IRS. The court affirmed that IRS administrative publications cannot override Supreme Court precedent.

**5. *Haynes v. United States*, 760 F. App'x 324 (5th Cir. 2019) (unpublished).** Remanded the issue and declined to establish a categorical rule on whether *Boyle* sweeps in all e-filing operational mechanics, noting that the question remained open. As an unpublished decision, it is not binding precedent in the Fifth Circuit.

**6. *Intress v. United States*, 404 F. Supp. 3d 1174 (M.D. Tenn. 2019).** Concluded that the mechanical act of e-filing is ministerial and falls within the scope of the *Boyle* non-delegable duty rule.

**7. Internal Revenue Manual 20.1.1.** Outlines administrative relief mechanisms, including First-Time Abate (IRM 20.1.1.3.3.2.1). FTA is an administrative waiver resting on a three-year history of clean compliance and operates independently of the legal reasonable cause defense.

## Analysis

The core tension turns on whether the transition from paper filing to electronic transmission alters the ministerial nature of the filing duty established in *United States v. Boyle*.

The taxpayer argues that e-filing requires a specialized electronic intermediary platform, removing the transmission mechanism from the direct control of an ordinary business person. However, as articulated by the Eleventh Circuit in *Lee*, the statutory deadline itself remains a fixed and certain point in time. The execution of Form 8879 represents a delegation of a mechanical task rather than reliance on a substantive legal interpretation.

While *Boyle* preserves an escape hatch for substantive advice, such as an accountant erroneously advising a client that an entity type is exempt from filing, it expressly bars relief for delegated deadlines. Because the taxpayer here simply relied on the preparer to complete the electronic transmission, the duty is properly categorized as ministerial.

Following the weight of authority in *Lee* and *Intress*, the signature on Form 8879 does not discharge the taxpayer's personal duty to verify acceptance. Unless the matter arises in a jurisdiction such as the Fifth Circuit, where *Haynes* left operational boundaries open to factual dispute, a preparer's mechanical failure to transmit does not satisfy the definition of reasonable cause under Treasury Regulation § 301.6651-1(c)(1).

## Conclusion

Relying on a preparer to electronically transmit a return is a ministerial task that cannot establish reasonable cause under *Boyle* where the deadline is missed. The taxpayer's stronger remedy is administrative First-Time Abate relief under IRM 20.1.1, subject to the eligibility conditions noted below, rather than litigating a reasonable cause defense.

## What would change the answer

- **If the preparer advised that no return was due.** *Boyle*'s preserved exception covers reliance on substantive professional advice on a question of law. Advice that no filing obligation existed is categorically different from a delegated deadline, and the analysis would turn on the reasonableness of that advice rather than on who pressed transmit.
- **If the return was rejected rather than never transmitted.** A rejected e-file generates an IRS acknowledgment. That changes what the taxpayer could have discovered and when, and it is the factual territory *Haynes* left open.
- **If the taxpayer was incapacitated during the filing period.** Illness or incapacity is an independent reasonable cause ground under IRM 20.1.1 and does not depend on the agency analysis at all.
- **If more than one tax period is involved.** First-Time Abate is generally available for a single period. A multi-year failure, as in *Lee*, cannot be resolved administratively and forces the reasonable cause argument this memo concludes is weak.
- **If the penalty assessed is failure to pay under § 6651(a)(2)** rather than failure to file. That is a separate penalty resting on separate facts and is not analyzed here.

## What remains open

- **The taxpayer's circuit is not specified in these facts, and it may determine the outcome.** *Lee* binds the Eleventh Circuit. *Haynes*, an unpublished decision, declined to decide the question in the Fifth. The analysis above follows the weight of authority; a Fifth Circuit taxpayer retains an argument that has not been foreclosed, though not a strong one.
- **First-Time Abate eligibility cannot be confirmed from these facts.** It requires a clean compliance history for the three preceding years, and that the taxpayer has filed and has paid or arranged to pay the tax due. Account transcripts for the three prior years would need to be obtained before FTA is recommended.
- **Whether the preparer's conduct supports a separate malpractice claim** is outside the scope of this memo. Such a claim was pursued and settled in *Lee*.
