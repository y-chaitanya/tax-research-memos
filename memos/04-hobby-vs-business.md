# Memorandum 4 — Hobby or Business: Profit Motive Under §183

**Prepared by:** Chaitanya Yarlagadda, EA
**Date:** September 2026
**Tax year:** 2026

---

## Facts

Client is a full-time software engineer earning $165,000 in W-2 wages. Since 2021 she has also operated a photography activity, reported on Schedule C.

Results reported:

| Year | Gross receipts | Expenses | Net |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | $2,400 | $11,800 | ($9,400) |
| 2022 | $4,100 | $12,600 | ($8,500) |
| 2023 | $6,800 | $13,200 | ($6,400) |
| 2024 | $9,500 | $12,100 | ($2,600) |
| 2025 | $14,200 | $13,400 | $800 |
| 2026 | $21,000 | $15,500 | $5,500 |

Additional facts:

- She maintains a separate business bank account and books in QuickBooks.
- She has a written business plan, updated in 2023 after reviewing why margins were negative.
- She raised her session rates twice and dropped a low-margin product line after the 2023 review.
- She has a business license and carries liability insurance.
- She advertises through a website and paid social media, and has repeat clients.
- She photographs weddings and corporate events, which she describes as demanding work rather than recreation.
- Equipment is not appreciating; she expects no gain from eventual sale.
- She spends roughly 15 hours per week on the activity, mostly weekends.
- She has no prior training in photography or business but took two business courses in 2022.

## Issue

Is the photography activity engaged in for profit under §183, so that expenses are deductible on Schedule C? If not, what is the consequence?

## Rule

§183(a) provides that if an activity is not engaged in for profit, no deduction attributable to that activity is allowed except as provided in §183(b).

§183(d) establishes a **presumption**: if gross income exceeds deductions for **three or more of five consecutive tax years** ending with the tax year in question, the activity is presumed to be engaged in for profit. The taxpayer bears the burden of showing profit motive when the presumption is not met.

Reg. §1.183-2(b) sets out **nine factors** relevant to whether an activity is engaged in for profit:

1. The manner in which the taxpayer carries on the activity
2. The expertise of the taxpayer or her advisors
3. The time and effort expended
4. The expectation that assets used may appreciate in value
5. The taxpayer's success in carrying on other similar or dissimilar activities
6. The taxpayer's history of income or losses with respect to the activity
7. The amount of occasional profits, if any
8. The financial status of the taxpayer
9. Elements of personal pleasure or recreation

No single factor is determinative, and the determination does not rest on a count of factors. All facts and circumstances are considered.

**Consequence of hobby classification.** Under current law, miscellaneous itemized deductions subject to the 2% floor are disallowed. The practical result is that hobby income is included in gross income while hobby expenses produce **no deduction at all**. This is materially harsher than the pre-2018 treatment, under which expenses were deductible up to the amount of hobby income as a miscellaneous itemized deduction.

## Analysis

### The §183(d) presumption is not yet met, but is close

The five-year window ending with 2026 is 2022 through 2026. Within that window the activity was profitable in 2025 and 2026 — **two years**, not three.

If 2027 is profitable, the window 2023–2027 will contain three profitable years and the presumption will apply for 2027.

For 2026, the client must establish profit motive under the facts and circumstances. The presumption is a safe harbor, not the standard. Failing it does not mean the activity is a hobby.

### Applying the nine factors

**1. Manner of carrying on the activity — strongly favorable.** Separate bank account, QuickBooks records, business license, liability insurance, and a written business plan. This is the factor that carries the most weight in practice, and the client is in a good position on it.

**2. Expertise — mixed.** No formal photography training and no prior business background. Two business courses in 2022 helps. The regulation looks to preparation through study or consultation with experts; the file would be stronger with evidence of industry research, mentorship, or consultation.

**3. Time and effort — favorable.** Fifteen hours per week alongside full-time employment is substantial. The regulation treats the withdrawal from another occupation as one indicator, which does not apply here, but sustained effort over five years does.

**4. Expectation of asset appreciation — unfavorable.** Camera equipment depreciates. There is no appreciating asset to supply a profit expectation. This factor weighs against, but it weighs against nearly every service business and is not strong evidence standing alone.

**5. Success in other activities — neutral.** No comparable history offered.

**6. History of income and losses — favorable on the trend, unfavorable on the aggregate.** Four consecutive loss years is the fact an examiner will lead with. But losses declined every single year — $9,400, $8,500, $6,400, $2,600 — and the activity then turned profitable and grew. Reg. §1.183-2(b)(6) expressly recognizes that losses in the startup phase of an activity do not necessarily indicate the absence of profit motive. The pattern here is a business climbing out of startup losses, not a hobby that never intended to make money.

**7. Amount of occasional profits — favorable and improving.** $800, then $5,500. Small in absolute terms, but the direction and rate of change matter. Gross receipts grew from $2,400 to $21,000 over five years, roughly nine-fold.

**8. Financial status — unfavorable.** W-2 income of $165,000 means the losses sheltered substantial other income. This factor weighs against the client and is the one that most often draws examination attention.

**9. Personal pleasure or recreation — favorable.** Wedding and corporate event photography is client-driven, deadline-bound work performed to someone else's specification. It is not the profile of a recreational activity. Had the facts involved landscape or wildlife photography sold occasionally, this factor would look very different.

### The factor that actually carries this engagement

The strongest evidence is not on the list in isolation. It is the **2023 business plan review followed by concrete operational changes**: raising rates twice and dropping a low-margin product line, after which losses narrowed and then reversed.

That sequence goes directly to factor 1, the manner of carrying on the activity, and it is the behavior of someone trying to make money. Reg. §1.183-2(b)(1) specifically identifies a change of operating methods adopted to improve profitability as an indicator of profit motive.

Documenting that sequence — the plan, the review, the decisions, and the results that followed — is the single most valuable thing in this file.

### Weighing the factors

Against the client: four loss years, high W-2 income, no appreciating assets, limited formal expertise.

For the client: businesslike operation, documented plan with responsive changes, substantial sustained time, steadily narrowing losses followed by growing profits, and work that is not recreational in character.

The combination of a documented course correction and the resulting reversal from loss to profit is the pattern the regulations contemplate for a genuine startup. The position that this is an activity engaged in for profit is well supported for 2026.

### What is at stake

If the activity were recharacterized as a hobby, the $21,000 of 2026 gross receipts would be included in income with **no offsetting deduction** for the $15,500 of expenses. The client would pay tax on $21,000 of gross receipts against an activity that netted $5,500.

Prior years are worse. Recharacterizing 2021 through 2024 would disallow approximately $49,700 of cumulative losses that offset W-2 income.

## Conclusion

1. **The §183(d) presumption does not apply for 2026** — only two of the five years ending in 2026 were profitable. If 2027 is profitable, the presumption applies for that year.
2. **The activity is nonetheless supportable as engaged in for profit** under the Reg. §1.183-2(b) factors, based on businesslike operation, a documented plan with responsive operational changes, sustained effort, and a clear trajectory from narrowing losses to growing profits.
3. **Continue reporting on Schedule C for 2026.**
4. **Document the file now**, while the facts are fresh: the business plan and its 2023 revision, the rate increases, the discontinued product line, and the financial results that followed each decision.
5. **Advise the client of the consequence of recharacterization** — gross income taxed with no deduction for expenses — so she understands why the recordkeeping matters.

## What would change the answer

- If 2027 returns to a loss, the presumption recedes further and the facts-and-circumstances analysis becomes harder each year.
- If the client could not produce the business plan and the record of operational changes, factor 1 would weaken considerably and the position would be materially less supportable.
- If the photography were personal in subject matter and sold only incidentally, factor 9 would shift against the client.
- If the client's W-2 income were modest, factor 8 would be neutral rather than unfavorable.

## Open items

- Obtain and retain copies of the business plan and its 2023 revision
- Document the rate increases and the discontinued product line with dates
- Confirm the client is tracking 2027 results against the plan
- Consider whether a §183(e) election to postpone determination is appropriate if the profit trend reverses
