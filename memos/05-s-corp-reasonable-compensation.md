# Memorandum 5 — Reasonable Compensation for an S Corporation Shareholder-Employee

**Prepared by:** Chaitanya Yarlagadda, EA
**Date:** September 2026
**Tax year:** 2026

---

## Facts

Client is the sole shareholder of an S corporation providing information
technology services to business customers. The corporation is a California
corporation and the shareholder is a California resident.

For 2026 the corporation expects:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross receipts | $520,000 |
| Operating expenses, including two part-time administrative employees | $260,000 |
| Net income before any officer compensation | $260,000 |

The shareholder works approximately fifty hours a week, performs all of the
billable work, and is the corporation's only revenue producer. The two
administrative employees do not generate revenue. The corporation owns modest
equipment and holds no significant capital investment.

To date in 2026 the shareholder has taken **$30,000 in W-2 wages** and
**$200,000 in distributions**, and intends to continue on that pattern.

The shareholder's stated reason for the low salary is that an accountant advised
years ago that a low salary "saves payroll tax."

## Issue

Is $30,000 defensible as reasonable compensation for this shareholder-employee?
If not, how should a supportable figure be determined and documented, and what
is the exposure if the current figure is left in place?

## Rule

### The shareholder is an employee

Reg. §31.3121(d)-1(b) provides that a corporate officer is an employee of the
corporation, except an officer who performs no services or only minor services
and neither receives nor is entitled to receive remuneration. An officer who
performs substantial services is an employee, and amounts paid for those
services are wages subject to employment taxes.

### Distributions are not self-employment income — which is why the rule exists

A shareholder's pro rata share of S corporation income is not self-employment
income. Rev. Rul. 59-221. That is the feature the low-salary strategy is built
on, and it is why the Service scrutinises the split between wages and
distributions.

### Payments in lieu of compensation are recharacterized

Rev. Rul. 74-44 holds that where an S corporation pays distributions to
shareholders in lieu of reasonable compensation for services, those amounts are
recharacterized as wages subject to employment taxes.

The courts have applied this consistently against shareholders who took no
salary or a nominal one:

- *Radtke v. United States*, 895 F.2d 1196 (7th Cir. 1990) — sole
  shareholder-attorney took no salary; dividends recharacterized as wages.
- *Spicer Accounting, Inc. v. United States*, 918 F.2d 90 (9th Cir. 1990) —
  same result in the Ninth Circuit, which governs California.
- *David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States*, 668 F.3d 1008 (8th Cir. 2012) —
  accountant took $24,000 against roughly $375,000 of distributions;
  compensation was reset to $91,044 on the strength of an expert valuation of
  the services rendered.
- *Glass Blocks Unlimited v. Commissioner*, T.C. Memo. 2013-180 — payments to a
  sole shareholder were wages notwithstanding the corporation's losses.

*Watson* matters most here. The court did not hold that the shareholder had to
be paid everything; it held that the salary had to reflect the value of the
services actually performed, and it accepted a figure supported by evidence.

### Deductibility

§162(a)(1) allows a deduction for a reasonable allowance for salaries or other
compensation for personal services actually rendered. In the closely held C
corporation cases the dispute is usually that compensation is too high. In S
corporations the dispute is nearly always that it is too low.

### Family members

§1366(e) permits the Service to adjust items among family members to reflect the
value of services rendered, where a family member performs services and is not
reasonably compensated. Not in issue on these facts, but it is the companion
rule and it should be checked whenever a spouse or child appears on the payroll.

### Factors

The Service's published factors (Fact Sheet 2008-25) include: training and
experience; duties and responsibilities; time and effort devoted to the
business; dividend history; payments to non-shareholder employees; the timing
and manner of paying bonuses; what comparable businesses pay for similar
services; compensation agreements; and the use of a formula to determine
compensation.

## Analysis

### $30,000 is not defensible on these facts

The shareholder performs all of the billable work in a business that generated
$520,000 of receipts. Compensation of $30,000 against $200,000 of distributions
implies that roughly thirteen percent of the value delivered is attributable to
services and the rest to capital, in a business with almost no capital.

That is the fact pattern in *Radtke*, *Spicer* and *Watson*, and it lost each
time. The advice the client received is not a position; it is the thing the
authority was written to address.

### The right question is what the services are worth

The analysis is not "what percentage of profit should be salary." No percentage
appears in the statute, the regulations, or the cases, and rules of thumb — a
60/40 split, "take half" — have no authority behind them. They are also the
first thing an examiner will recognise.

The supportable approach is to value the services:

1. **Identify what the shareholder actually does**, hour by hour and role by
   role. Billable technical work, sales, client management and administration
   are different roles with different market rates. A shareholder doing all four
   is holding several jobs.
2. **Price each role against market data** — Bureau of Labor Statistics
   occupational wage data for the metropolitan area, industry salary surveys, or
   what the corporation would have to pay an employee to do that work.
3. **Sum, and sanity-check against the profit available.** If the total exceeds
   what the business earns, the business is not profitable and the figure comes
   down. Reasonable compensation is capped by economic reality.
4. **Record the analysis contemporaneously**, in a written compensation study
   adopted by board resolution, before the wages are paid rather than after they
   are questioned.

*Watson* is the model: the taxpayer lost, but the figure the court accepted came
from an expert valuation of services. A client who does that work in advance is
not in the same position as one who picks a number.

### The exposure if nothing changes

Recharacterization does not produce a small adjustment. On the additional wages
the corporation and shareholder owe the combined employer and employee FICA —
6.2% each for social security up to the wage base, and 1.45% each for Medicare
with no ceiling — plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax above the applicable
threshold, plus federal and California unemployment tax. Confirm the 2026 social
security wage base and Additional Medicare Tax threshold before quantifying.

Penalties follow the tax: failure to deposit under §6656, failure to file
employment tax returns under §6651, and a potential accuracy-related penalty
under §6662. Interest runs from the original due dates.

The offsetting deduction is real but incomplete. The corporation deducts the
additional wages and the employer share, which reduces the pass-through income —
so the income tax effect is largely a wash. The employment tax is not.

### The §199A tension, and why it may not apply here

Wages reduce qualified business income, so raising the salary reduces the §199A
deduction. For a taxpayer whose income exceeds the threshold, wages also feed
the W-2 wage limitation of §199A(b)(2)(B), which can increase the allowable
deduction. Those two effects run in opposite directions and there is an
optimising computation, not a rule of thumb.

Before running it, a threshold question applies to this client. §199A(d)(2)(A)
excludes a specified service trade or business, which includes consulting.
Reg. §1.199A-5(b)(2)(vii) defines consulting as providing professional advice
and counsel, and excludes services ancillary to the sale of goods and services
that are not advisory in nature. Whether this corporation's IT work is
"consulting" for this purpose turns on what it actually does — advising clients
on technology decisions is likely within it; installing, maintaining and
supporting systems under a service contract may not be.

If the business is an SSTB and the shareholder's taxable income exceeds the
phase-out range, there is no §199A deduction, and the wage-optimisation analysis
falls away entirely. That question should be settled before any compensation
modelling is done.

### California

California does not follow the federal treatment in two respects that matter
here. The S corporation is subject to the California franchise tax at 1.5% of
net income, with the $800 minimum, so additional wages reduce the California
franchise tax base as well as the federal pass-through income. California also
does not allow a §199A-equivalent deduction, so the §199A tension described
above has no California counterpart.

Wages are subject to California employment taxes — unemployment insurance,
employment training tax and state disability insurance — which should be
included when the incremental cost of raising the salary is quantified.

### Timing

Compensation can be corrected prospectively for the remainder of 2026 through
payroll, which is straightforward. Correcting a year that has closed means
amended employment tax returns, amended Forms W-2, and an amended Form 1120-S
and Schedule K-1. The cost of fixing this rises sharply at year end, which is a
practical reason to raise it now rather than at the return.

## Conclusion

1. **$30,000 is not supportable.** The shareholder performs all of the revenue-
   producing work in a business with negligible capital. On *Radtke*, *Spicer*
   and *Watson*, a nominal salary against substantial distributions is the fact
   pattern that loses.
2. **Commission a documented compensation study** valuing the shareholder's
   roles against market data, and adopt the resulting figure by board resolution
   before the wages are paid.
3. **Correct prospectively for the balance of 2026** through payroll rather than
   at the return.
4. **Settle the SSTB question first.** If the corporation is a specified service
   trade or business and income exceeds the phase-out range, §199A does not apply
   and does not belong in the compensation analysis at all.
5. **Quantify the California cost** alongside the federal, including the 1.5%
   franchise tax effect and state payroll taxes.
6. The client should understand that the objective is a figure that can be
   defended with evidence — not the lowest figure that has not yet been
   questioned.

## What would change the answer

- If the corporation held substantial capital — equipment, inventory, or
  employees whose work generates profit independently of the shareholder — a
  larger share of the profit would be attributable to capital, and a lower
  compensation figure would be supportable.
- If the shareholder worked part-time, or one of the administrative employees
  produced revenue, the valuation of the shareholder's services would fall.
- If the corporation operated at a loss, reasonable compensation would be capped
  by what the business can pay; *Glass Blocks* shows that this does not reduce it
  to zero where payments were in fact made to the shareholder.
- If a spouse or child were on the payroll, §1366(e) would need to be addressed
  in the same analysis.
- If the shareholder were a passive owner performing no services, no
  compensation would be required at all — but then the distributions would not
  be at risk either.
